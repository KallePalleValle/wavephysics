A Michelson interferometer splits a single beam into two with a beam splitter, sends each half down a separate arm to a mirror, and recombines them at the detector. The sketch above shows the layout: light from the source meets the beam splitter (BS), goes to mirror M₁ along arm 1 and to mirror M₂ along arm 2, and returns to interfere on the detector.

From the detector's point of view, each mirror creates a virtual image of the source — call them S₁ and S₂. What the detector sees is therefore the interference of two spherical waves coming from these two virtual sources. The middle figure visualizes this: the wavefronts from S₁ and S₂ overlap, and where they meet in phase the detector is bright; where they meet out of phase it is dark. The two wavelengths must match — pairs of bright fringes are separated by one wavelength of path difference.

When the two arm lengths are equal, S₁ and S₂ coincide and the detector is uniformly bright. As ΔL = L₁ − L₂ grows, the second arm shortens and S₂ shifts back along the optical axis. Points on the detector at the same distance from both sources form concentric circles, giving the famous ring pattern shown on the right. The fringe spacing depends on the wavelength λ and on ΔL. Tilting M₂ — the lateral-offset slider — shifts S₂ sideways: the rings drift off-axis and eventually become nearly straight fringes, exactly what an experimenter sees when the interferometer is not perfectly aligned. This is often done on purpose for easier redings.
